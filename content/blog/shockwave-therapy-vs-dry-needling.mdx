## Shockwave Therapy vs Dry Needling: The Short Answer

Shockwave therapy uses acoustic wave pulses applied through the skin. It is most often used for stubborn tendon and attachment-site pain. Dry needling inserts thin needles into tight muscle spots called trigger points. It is most often used for muscle knots and referred tightness.

The research does not crown a clear winner on pain. The better question is what is actually driving your pain, and what you will change in how you move.

Here is the quick split:

- **Muscle knot or tight band:** dry needling or hands-on myofascial release therapy are the usual options.
- **Chronic tendon or heel pain:** shockwave therapy is the usual option.
- **Afraid of needles:** shockwave therapy and hands-on care avoid them.
- **Pain that keeps returning:** neither works alone. Loading and movement retraining matter.

## What Shockwave Therapy Is and How It Works

Shockwave therapy, also called extracorporeal shockwave therapy (ESWT), delivers acoustic wave pulses into sore tissue through a handheld applicator. The clinician moves the applicator over the painful area while you stay clothed or lightly exposed, depending on the site.

The goal is to stimulate a stubborn area that has stopped adapting well. It is not a cure. It is a way to help the tissue tolerate the loading and movement that rebuild it.

### Radial vs Focused

Shockwave comes in two broad types. Radial shockwave is pneumatic, so the pulses spread outward from the applicator. Focused shockwave concentrates energy at a set depth.

The distinction matters when you read studies. One registered protocol for neck pain notes that [evidence for focused shockwave in short-term neck pain is lacking](https://cdn.clinicaltrials.gov/large-docs/72/NCT07266272/Prot_SAP_ICF_000.pdf). Results from one type or body area do not automatically transfer to another.

### What It Is Used For

Shockwave therapy is most often used for long-standing tendon and attachment-site problems. Common examples include:

- Plantar fascia and heel pain
- Tennis elbow and other elbow tendon pain
- Knee tendon pain, such as jumper's knee
- Achilles tendon pain in runners

For more on specific conditions, see [what shockwave therapy is used for](https://www.movemuscleandjoint.com/blog/what-is-shockwave-therapy-used-for), [shockwave therapy for Achilles tendonitis](https://www.movemuscleandjoint.com/blog/shockwave-therapy-for-achilles-tendonitis) and [shockwave therapy for plantar fasciitis in Overland Park](https://www.movemuscleandjoint.com/blog/shockwave-therapy-for-plantar-fasciitis-overland-park).

### What a Session Feels Like

Expect rapid pressure pulses, a tapping or thumping sensation, and some tenderness over sore spots. Many people describe it as intense but short. The applicator keeps moving, and your provider can adjust intensity.

A session is brief, and you walk out afterward. Some people notice mild soreness later. Our guide to [shockwave therapy side effects](https://www.movemuscleandjoint.com/blog/shockwave-therapy-side-effects) explains what is normal.

One small pilot study in quadriceps trigger points found that [radial shockwave did not cause post-treatment soreness, unlike dry needling](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1360859218300780). That is a small study, so treat it as a hint, not a rule.

## What Dry Needling Is and How It Works

Dry needling uses thin, solid needles, with no medication in them, inserted into muscle. The target is usually a trigger point, a tender, tight spot in a muscle that can refer ache to other areas.

It is mainly used for myofascial trigger points, and [evidence on its effectiveness is described as conflicting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dry_needling). That is worth knowing before you assume it is a sure fix.

### Trigger Points and the Twitch Response

When the needle reaches a sensitive spot, the muscle can give a brief involuntary contraction, called a local twitch response. Some providers aim for it deliberately. Others use gentler techniques without chasing it.

In a tennis elbow meta-analysis, [trigger point dry needling that produced a local twitch response improved pain within one week](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0003999324008232). The mean difference was about 1.09 points lower on the pain scale.

### What It Is Used For

Dry needling is usually offered for:

- Muscle knots in the neck, shoulder blade or upper back
- Tight calves, hips or glutes that limit motion
- Referred ache that seems to come from a tender muscle band
- Muscle-related pain around a tendon problem

### Soreness Afterward

The sensation is often a small prick, then a deep ache or cramp-like feeling. Soreness can follow for a day or so.

In that same quadriceps pilot study, pressure pain threshold dropped at 30 minutes and at 24 hours after needling, then rose at 72 hours. In plain terms, the area can feel more sensitive before it settles.

Rules on who may perform dry needling vary by state. If you are considering it, ask the provider about their training and check your state's rules.

## Key Differences Side by Side

The two treatments differ in what they target, how they reach it and what you feel. Here is the comparison, point by point.

### Tissue Targeted

Dry needling is aimed at muscle, specifically trigger points. Shockwave therapy is most often aimed at tendon and the area where tendon meets bone, though it is also used on tight muscle spots.

That overlap is why the two get compared. Real pain does not always sort neatly into muscle or tendon.

### Invasive vs Non-Invasive

Dry needling breaks the skin. Shockwave therapy does not. For some people that settles the choice, and for others it does not matter at all.

Both are generally considered low-risk when done by a trained provider after a proper screening. Screening is the key point, and it is covered in the safety section below.

### Sensation and Downtime

Shockwave feels like pressure pulses and a tapping tenderness. Dry needling feels like a prick and then an ache. Neither usually requires time off, though you may want to go easy on the area for a day.

### Typical Number of Sessions

Protocols vary widely, so be wary of any page promising a fixed number. Two published examples show the range:

- **Iliotibial band trial:** In a [randomized trial with 20 people per group](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/357690676_Shockwave_Therapy_Versus_Dry_Needling_for_the_Management_of_Iliotibial_Band_Syndrome_A_Randomized_Clinical_Trial), dry needling lasted 15 minutes, twice a week for 4 weeks. Shockwave was 500 pulses weekly for 4 weeks, plus 2,000 pulses at 3 trigger points.
- **Jumper's knee trial:** A [registered trial](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT07756918) plans 3 to 5 shockwave sessions at low to medium energy (0.08 to 0.28 mJ/mm²). Its dry needling arm gets 3 sessions a week.

Your own plan depends on the tissue, how long it has been painful and how you respond.

### Who Should Avoid Each

Safety screening matters for both. A registry entry for a needling trial lists [warfarin, a blood coagulation disorder and local infection](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT04348097) among its exclusions. If you take blood thinners or have a bleeding condition, say so before any needling.

For shockwave therapy, tell your provider about your full health history, any implants, and any recent injections or injuries near the area. A good provider screens first and tells you why a treatment is or is not appropriate.

## What the Research Says

The evidence is more modest than many clinic pages suggest. Here is what we can say honestly.

### Head-to-Head Results

A meta-analysis of 9 randomized trials in tendinopathy found that [shockwave and needling alone gave comparable pain relief](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12265909/). That does not make them identical, but it means neither is clearly stronger for pain in the pooled data.

A single iliotibial band trial, [published in a peer-reviewed journal](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9260514/), also compared the two directly in a small group. Small trials like this are useful, but they do not settle the question for everyone.

### Combining the Two

The same tendinopathy review found that combining shockwave and needling beat either alone for delayed pain reduction. Three studies with 161 subjects showed shockwave plus dry needling beat shockwave alone on delayed pain, by about 1.8 points on the pain scale.

The takeaway is not that you need both. It is that treating the tendon and the surrounding muscle together can help. At our clinic, we do this with shockwave therapy and myofascial release therapy rather than needling.

### Limits of the Evidence

Here is where to stay cautious:

- Trials are often small and use different protocols.
- Results for one condition may not apply to another.
- Radial and focused shockwave are not interchangeable.
- Pain scores at the end of treatment do not tell you about long-term function.

I would treat any claim that one option is simply better with caution. The honest answer is that the right choice depends on you.

Use these as starting points, not diagnoses. A hands-on assessment sorts out what is really going on.

We cover this in [shockwave therapy benefits for chronic tendon and muscle pain](https://www.movemuscleandjoint.com/blog/shockwave-therapy-benefits).

### Needle Anxiety

Fear of needles is a real decision factor, and you should not have to push through it. A non-needle option such as shockwave therapy or hands-on care is a perfectly reasonable choice if needles make you tense.

### Pain That Is Not Improving

If you have tried a treatment for several visits with no change, the issue may be the diagnosis or the plan, not your effort. Ask your provider what they expect to change by when, and what they will do if it does not.

Questions to bring to any provider:

1. What do you think is the source of my pain, muscle or tendon?
2. Why this treatment and not the other?
3. How many visits do you expect, and how will we know it is working?
4. What should I do between visits?
5. What are the risks for someone with my health history?

## Why Neither Works Alone

This is the part most comparison pages skip. A treatment can calm pain or stimulate tissue, but your tendon or muscle still has to cope with running, lifting, sitting and parenting.

That is where **loading** and **movement retraining** matter. Tendons generally need progressive, controlled load to adapt. Muscles that stay tight often have a reason, such as weak support nearby or a joint that is not moving well.

A sensible plan usually looks like this:

- Find the source of pain through a movement assessment.
- Use a treatment, such as shockwave therapy or myofascial release therapy, to make movement easier.
- Add [targeted rehab](https://www.movemuscleandjoint.com/services/targeted-rehab) to build capacity in the tissue.
- Adjust training, desk setup or daily habits that keep irritating the area.

## When to See a Doctor Promptly

Muscle and tendon pain is common, but some symptoms need prompt medical attention rather than a treatment comparison. See a doctor promptly if you have:

- Numbness, tingling or new weakness in an arm or leg
- Loss of bladder or bowel control
- Fever with pain, or redness, heat and swelling in the area
- Pain after a significant fall or accident
- Severe night pain that does not ease with rest

These can point to problems that need a different kind of care. If you are unsure, it is always fine to ask.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is shockwave therapy better than dry needling?

Neither is clearly better for pain. A meta-analysis of randomized trials in tendinopathy found comparable pain relief from each alone. The better fit depends on whether your pain comes from a muscle trigger point or a stubborn tendon, which an assessment can sort out.

### Does shockwave therapy hurt more than dry needling?

They feel different. Shockwave feels like pressure pulses and tenderness, while dry needling feels like a prick followed by an ache. In one small pilot study, radial shockwave did not cause post-treatment soreness, unlike dry needling, but one small study does not predict your experience.

### Can you combine shockwave therapy and dry needling?

Research suggests the combination can help with delayed pain reduction in tendinopathy compared with either alone. At Move Muscle & Joint, we do not offer dry needling. We pair shockwave therapy with myofascial release therapy and targeted rehab when your case calls for it.

### Is shockwave a good option if I am afraid of needles?

Yes. Shockwave therapy uses acoustic wave pulses through the skin and does not use needles. It is a reasonable choice if needles make you anxious, provided your provider confirms it suits your condition.

### Does dry needling release muscle knots permanently?

No one can promise permanent results. Evidence on dry needling is described as conflicting, and tight muscles often return if the cause is not addressed. Mobility work, loading and changes to daily habits help the effect last.

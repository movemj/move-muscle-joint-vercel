## Does Myofascial Release Help Migraines?

Myofascial release (MFR) has the strongest support for tension-type and neck-related (cervicogenic) headaches. For migraine, the research is mixed. A [2024 systematic review and meta-analysis](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10999287/) searched eight databases and looked at MFR for all three headache types.

The authors concluded that MFR can significantly reduce pain and disability in tension-type and cervicogenic headache. For migraine, [the results were inconsistent](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1155/2024/2042069).

That split matters. Many pages blur these headaches together and promise relief for all of them. I would rather give you the real picture so you can decide with clear expectations.

### Tension-Type and Cervicogenic Headache

Tension-type headache is usually a pressing or tightening ache on both sides of the head, with no known medical cause. Cervicogenic headache starts in the neck and spreads upward.

In the 2024 review, the pain reduction for cervicogenic headache was large (SMD -2.01, 95% CI -2.73 to -1.29). For tension-type headache it was moderate (SMD -0.86). Those numbers describe group averages, not what any one person will feel.

A randomized trial of tension headache, summarized in [a review of MFR trials](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/263091881_Effectiveness_of_myofascial_release_Systematic_review_of_randomized_controlled_trials), compared two MFR styles with a control of slow, soft stroking:

- Headache days per four weeks fell by 7.1 with direct MFR and 6.7 with indirect MFR.
- The control group dropped by 1.6.
- By weeks 17 to 20, headache frequency was down 59.2% (direct), 54% (indirect) and 13.3% (control).

### Migraine

Migraine is a neurological condition, and MFR is not a migraine treatment in the way medication is. Still, neck and shoulder tension often travel with migraine, and that is where hands-on care may help.

A 2025 pilot study used compression of trigger points, mainly in the upper trapezius, in people with migraine. The authors concluded it [may give relief in episodic migraine](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12891-025-08360-1). It was small, had no control group and did not test MFR itself.

That paper also cites earlier work finding that most people with migraine have more active myofascial trigger points than usual. It notes that benefit after a trigger-point intervention in chronic migraine was unclear after one month.

### How Strong Is the Evidence?

Be cautious. The 2024 review rated most of the evidence as low or very low quality. Only the disability improvement for tension-type and cervicogenic headache reached moderate quality.

A separate [review of physical therapy for tension-type headache](https://www.mdpi.com/1660-4601/20/5/4466) found that both direct and indirect MFR significantly reduced headache frequency at three months. It also rated the overall evidence as very low quality.

So the fair summary is promising, not proven. MFR looks worth trying for neck-driven headaches, especially alongside exercise, and it should not replace medical care for migraine.

## What Myofascial Release Therapy Is (and Isn't)

Fascia is the thin connective tissue that wraps muscles, joints and nerves. Myofascial release therapy uses gentle, sustained pressure and stretching to reduce tension in that tissue and the muscles beneath it.

It is not a cure for headaches, and it does not "eliminate" trigger points for good. It is a way to reduce tension and restore movement so your neck and shoulders work better.

### Direct and Indirect Techniques

Direct MFR applies firmer pressure into a restricted area and holds until the tissue softens. Indirect MFR uses lighter pressure and moves gently in the direction the tissue wants to go.

Both were tested in the tension headache trial above, and both outperformed the control. Your provider can choose based on how sensitive your neck and scalp are on the day.

### Vs. Deep Tissue Massage

Massage and MFR overlap, but they are not identical. MFR is slower and more targeted, with a focus on restrictions that limit movement. ## How Neck and Shoulder Tension Connect to Headaches

Your neck holds up your head all day. When the muscles at the base of the skull, the upper back and the shoulders tighten, they can refer pain upward into the head, behind the eyes or into the temples.

Desk work, phone use, lifting and long drives all load these muscles. That is why many readers in Overland Park, Leawood and Prairie Village notice headaches on busy workdays.

### Trigger Points in the Neck, Shoulders, Jaw and Scalp

Trigger points are tight, tender spots in muscle that can send pain to other areas. Common headache-related areas include:

- The upper trapezius, between your neck and shoulder
- The muscles at the base of the skull
- The jaw muscles you use to chew and clench
- The scalp and temple area

If pressing one of these spots reproduces your familiar headache, that is a useful clue for your provider.

### Migraine Vs. Tension-Type Vs. Cervicogenic

These headaches can feel alike, but they differ in key ways:

- **Tension-type:** a pressing or tightening ache on both sides, usually without nausea.
- **Cervicogenic:** starts in the neck, often one-sided, and worsens with certain neck positions.
- **Migraine:** often throbbing, may bring nausea or light and sound sensitivity, and can include aura.

Many people have more than one type. A proper assessment helps sort out which part is neck-driven and which is not.

## Who May Benefit, and When to See a Doctor First

MFR may suit you if your headaches come with neck or shoulder stiffness, get worse after desk time or lifting, or ease when you change position. It may also help if you want a drug-free option alongside what you already do.

It is less likely to be the main answer if your headaches follow a pure migraine pattern with no neck involvement. In that case, your doctor leads, and hands-on care plays a supporting role at most.

### See a Doctor Promptly If You Have

Some headaches need medical evaluation before any hands-on care. Please see a doctor promptly if you notice:

- A sudden, severe headache unlike any you have had
- Headache with fever, a stiff neck, confusion or a rash
- Numbness, weakness, vision loss or trouble speaking
- A headache after a head injury
- A new headache pattern that keeps getting worse

This list is general guidance, not a full medical checklist. If something feels wrong, get checked first.

## Combining Myofascial Release With Active Care

Hands-on work can calm tight tissue, but it does not change why the tissue got tight. If you sit with your head forward for eight hours, the tension tends to return.

That is why movement matters. Lasting results come from pairing release work with strength, mobility and better daily habits.

Targeted rehab for neck-linked headaches often includes:

- Short movement breaks for your workday

### Tracking Progress With a Headache Diary

A simple diary tells you whether the plan is working, which matters more than how a single session felt. Record these each day:

1. Whether you had a headache, and how strong it was from 0 to 10
2. How long it lasted
3. Neck stiffness and sleep that day
4. Hours at a desk or screen, and any training
5. Medication taken, if any

Check the pattern every two to four weeks. Fewer headache days, shorter episodes and better neck motion are the signs to look for.

## At-Home Options and Their Limits

You can do a lot at home for neck tension. Self-care is a good support, though it works best alongside an assessment that tells you where to focus.

Options worth trying:

- Gentle neck and upper back stretches several times a day
- A lacrosse ball or foam roller on the upper back and shoulders, with light pressure
- Heat on tight muscles before movement
- Regular breaks from sitting
- Consistent sleep and hydration

Go easy around the front and sides of the neck, and avoid hard pressure on the skull base. Massage guns and aggressive rolling can leave the area more irritated. If self-care does not change things in a few weeks, that is a good reason to get assessed.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can myofascial release help migraines?

It may help some people, mostly when neck and shoulder tension is part of the picture. The 2024 meta-analysis found inconsistent results for migraine itself. It is best seen as a supporting option alongside medical care.

### Does myofascial release work better for tension headaches than for migraines?

The research points that way. The 2024 review found significant pain and disability improvements for tension-type and cervicogenic headache, but mixed results for migraine. Most of the evidence was still low quality, so expect a trial period, not a guarantee.

### How long does it take to feel relief from myofascial release?

Neck motion and tightness may feel different within a few visits, but headache frequency tends to change more gradually. Trials tracked results over weeks and months. A headache diary helps you see the trend instead of judging one session.

### Can I do myofascial release at home?

You can use gentle stretching, light ball or roller work and heat on the upper back and shoulders. Avoid hard pressure on the neck and skull base. An assessment can show where to focus so home work supports your plan.

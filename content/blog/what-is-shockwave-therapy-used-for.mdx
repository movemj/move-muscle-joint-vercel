## What Is Shockwave Therapy Used For?

Shockwave therapy, also called extracorporeal shockwave therapy (ESWT), is a noninvasive treatment. It sends acoustic wave pulses through the skin to [stimulate healing](https://www.medicalnewstoday.com/articles/extracorporeal-shockwave-therapy) in sore tissue.

In musculoskeletal care it is used mostly for long-lasting tendon and soft tissue problems. The most common ones are:

- Plantar fasciitis (heel and arch pain)
- Tennis elbow and golfer's elbow
- Achilles tendinopathy
- Patellar tendinopathy (jumper's knee)
- Calcific tendonitis of the shoulder
- Retrocalcaneal bursitis
- Some stress fractures and fractures that fail to heal

Not all of these uses have the same level of proof. Later in this article I sort them into tiers so you know where the evidence is strong and where it is mixed.

## How Shockwave Therapy Works

Shockwave therapy did not start in sports medicine. It began in urology, where it was first used in Germany in 1980 to [break up kidney stones](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8944950/).

### How the Waves Work, in Plain Language

A handheld applicator delivers short bursts of acoustic energy to the painful area. Gel on the skin helps the waves travel into the tissue.

In orthopedics, the goal is different from kidney stones. The waves are not meant to break up tissue. They are meant to [induce tissue repair and regeneration](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3342893/).

Researchers say the exact mechanism is not fully understood. That is worth knowing. Shockwave therapy is a tool with a good track record for certain conditions, not a fully explained machine.

The working idea is that the pulses prompt the body to restart repair in tissue that has been stuck in a chronic, irritated state. Many tendon problems fit that description.

### Focused Vs. Radial Shockwave

You may see two terms when you read about ESWT. They describe how the energy travels.

- **Focused shockwave** penetrates deeper and at higher energy, aimed at a specific spot.
- **Radial shockwave** is more superficial and treats a wider area of tissue.

This difference is covered in [research on the spine](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7704023/) and in [a plantar fasciitis trial](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT04332471). It is one reason the right setup depends on what is being treated and how deep it sits.

You do not need to master the physics. You do need a clinician who can explain why the settings fit your problem.

## Conditions Shockwave Therapy Is Commonly Used For

Clinic and hospital pages often list every condition as if the proof were equal. It is not. Here is a more honest breakdown.

### Strongest Evidence: Plantar Fasciitis and Tennis Elbow

The FDA first approved ESWT devices for proximal plantar fasciitis in 2000. Approval for lateral epicondylitis (tennis elbow) followed in the [early 2000s](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3342893/).

A 2026 [NPR report](https://www.npr.org/2026/03/02/nx-s1-5721112/plantar-fasciitis-heel-pain-shockwave-therapy-excersise) also says the FDA approves it for plantar fasciitis and tennis elbow.

Plantar fasciitis is common. A trial listing reports it affects 3.8 per 1,000 people in the US. If you have stabbing heel pain with your first steps in the morning, you are far from alone. Our guide to [shockwave therapy for plantar fasciitis in Overland Park](https://www.movemuscleandjoint.com/blog/shockwave-therapy-for-plantar-fasciitis-overland-park) goes deeper.

The approved heel pain use is narrow. Insurance policies describe it as chronic proximal plantar fasciitis in patients 18 or older who have had [unsuccessful conservative therapy](https://medicalpolicy.bcbstx.com/content/dam/bcbs/medicalpolicy/pdf/surgery/SUR705.018_2024-11-15.pdf).

### Good Evidence: Achilles and Patellar Tendinopathy

NPR reports that ESWT is effective for Achilles tendinopathy. A [systematic review and meta-analysis](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10468604/) looked at patellar tendinopathy, Achilles tendinopathy and plantar fasciitis together.

That same review also notes that guidance on Achilles tendinopathy and plantar fasciitis conflicts. In plain terms, the research leans positive, but experts do not fully agree on where it fits.

Runners and jumpers often ask about these tendons. Our article on [shockwave therapy for Achilles tendonitis](https://www.movemuscleandjoint.com/blog/shockwave-therapy-for-achilles-tendonitis) covers what that looks like for runners.

Patellar tendinopathy, often called jumper's knee, shows up in basketball players, lifters and anyone who loads the knee hard and often.

### Mixed or Less Certain: Shoulder, Hamstring and Others

NPR describes results as mixed for shoulder tendonitis and hamstring injuries. Calcific shoulder tendonitis, where calcium deposits form in a tendon, is a commonly listed use at [WashU Orthopedics](https://www.ortho.wustl.edu/content/videos/114/shockwave-therapy-treatment-.aspx).

That same page also lists these uses:

- Retrocalcaneal bursitis
- Golfer's elbow
- Patellar tendinosis
- Chronic stress fractures
- Non-union fractures

Hospital programs such as [NewYork-Presbyterian](https://www.nyp.org/advances/article/extracorporeal-shockwave-therapy-offers-novel-approach-to-treating-tendon-and-bone-injuries) describe ESWT for tendon and bone injuries more broadly.

"Commonly listed" is not the same as "strongly proven." A good clinician will tell you which tier your problem sits in.

### Investigational Uses

You will also find shockwave therapy discussed for the spine, wounds and other areas. These uses appear in research, and the spine is covered in a [review of ESWT for the spine](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7704023/).

Treat them as investigational. That means the research is still developing and nobody should promise you an outcome. If a provider markets shockwave therapy as a fix for everything, be cautious.

## Evidence at a Glance

Here is a simple way to read the tiers without a chart:

1. **FDA-cleared uses:** plantar fasciitis and tennis elbow.
2. **Effective in published reviews:** Achilles tendinopathy, with some conflicting guidance.
3. **Mixed results:** shoulder tendonitis and hamstring injuries.
4. **Investigational:** spine, wounds and other off-label uses.

Why does this matter? Because hype hurts patients. If you know a use is mixed, you can decide whether to try it, how long to try, and what else to do alongside it.

## What Results Can You Expect?

Shockwave therapy is not a quick fix. It works with your body's repair process, and that takes time.

### How Many Sessions and How Long Until You Notice a Change

A typical course is [three to six sessions about a week apart](https://acibademinternational.com/blog/shockwave-therapy-how-it-works-and-which-conditions-respond/). That is a general range from one clinic source, and your plan depends on your condition.

Each session is short. One clinic describes them as about [5 to 10 minutes](https://www.blackberryclinic.co.uk/articles/what-to-expect-from-a-shockwave-treatment-session/).

The effect usually builds over weeks, not hours. One clinic reports significant benefit [6 to 8 weeks after treatment](https://complete-physio.co.uk/services/shockwave-therapy/). Treat that as one clinic's report, not a promise.

So a realistic way to think about it looks like this:

- You may feel little or some change right after a session.
- Improvement tends to build across the course of sessions.
- Full results often take weeks after the last visit.
- Some people respond well and some do not.

### Why Exercise and Rehab Alongside It Matter

Tendons get better when they are loaded in the right way. Shockwave therapy may help calm an irritated area, but your tendon still needs to get stronger.

NPR's headline pairs shockwave therapy with exercise for plantar fasciitis and elbow pain for this reason. The device is one part of the plan.

A good plan might include:

- Calf, foot or forearm strengthening, depending on the problem
- Changes to training volume, footwear or workstation setup
- Mobility work for the joints above and below the sore spot
- Myofascial release therapy for tight surrounding muscle

The goal is not just less pain this week. It is better movement and tissue that can handle your life, your training or your job. That is what lasting results depend on.

## What Happens During a Shockwave Session

Knowing the steps removes much of the worry. Here is what most sessions look like.

### Before Treatment

Your clinician asks about your pain, your history and what you have already tried. They examine the area and may look at how you move.

This step matters. Heel pain can come from the plantar fascia, but it can also come from other sources. Treating the wrong thing wastes your time.

### During Treatment

You sit or lie in a comfortable position. Gel goes on the skin, and the applicator delivers the acoustic wave pulses over the sore area.

Many people describe a tapping or pulsing sensation. Intensity can usually be adjusted. If it feels too strong, tell your clinician.

### After Treatment

There is little to no recovery time with this kind of treatment, so most people go straight back to their day. Your clinician will tell you how to handle exercise, running or lifting in the days after.

Ask for specific guidance. "Take it easy" is not a plan. You should know what you can do, what to avoid and when to return.

## Side Effects and Safety

Some pages claim shockwave therapy has no known side effects. That is not accurate, and you deserve the full picture.

### Common, Short-Lived Effects

The most common effects are soreness, redness and bruising. One clinic source says they [usually clear within one to three days](https://acibademinternational.com/blog/shockwave-therapy-how-it-works-and-which-conditions-respond/).

Sources that report these effects also mention a rare risk of tendon rupture. It is uncommon, but it is a reason to have a trained clinician choose settings and guide your activity.

For more detail, read our guide to [shockwave therapy side effects and what to expect](https://www.movemuscleandjoint.com/blog/shockwave-therapy-side-effects).

### Who Should Be Cautious

Shockwave therapy is not right for everyone, and rules differ by device and by condition. Tell your clinician about your full health history, current medications and any recent injections or surgery near the area.

A proper screening should happen before your first treatment. If a provider skips it, ask why.

### When to See a Doctor Promptly

Pain is not always a tendon problem. Get medical care promptly if you have:

- Numbness or tingling that spreads or does not go away
- New weakness in a leg, foot or arm
- Fever along with pain or swelling
- Loss of bladder or bowel control
- Severe pain after an injury, or you cannot bear weight
- A hot, red, swollen calf

These symptoms need a medical evaluation before any treatment plan.

## Is Shockwave Therapy FDA-Approved and Covered by Insurance?

Shockwave therapy is FDA-approved for certain uses, mainly chronic proximal plantar fasciitis and tennis elbow. Many other uses are called off-label or investigational.

Coverage is a separate question, and it is where many patients get surprised. ## Is Shockwave Therapy Right for You?

Shockwave therapy tends to fit best when the problem is a chronic tendon or soft tissue issue that has not settled with time and basic care. Wexner Medical Center notes that [patients do not have to try more conservative treatment first](https://wexnermedical.osu.edu/orthopedics/sports-medicine/treatments/shockwave-therapy), and that it can be used for acute and chronic pain.

That does not mean everyone should start there. Many people do well with movement, loading and hands-on care first, and some use shockwave therapy alongside those.

Before you book, ask any provider these questions:

1. What exactly is my diagnosis?
2. Where does shockwave therapy sit in the evidence for my condition?
3. How many sessions do you expect, and how will we measure progress?
4. What exercise and activity changes go with it?
5. What does it cost, and is it covered?
6. What will we do if it does not help?

The last question matters most. A good plan has a next step.

## Frequently asked questions

### Does shockwave therapy actually work?

For some conditions, yes. The evidence is strongest for plantar fasciitis and tennis elbow, and published reviews also lean positive for Achilles tendinopathy. Results are mixed for shoulder tendonitis and hamstring injuries, and not everyone responds.

### Is shockwave therapy painful?

Most people feel a tapping or pulsing sensation that can be uncomfortable over a sore spot. The intensity can usually be adjusted during the session. Sessions are short, often about 5 to 10 minutes.

### How many sessions will I need?

A typical course is three to six sessions about a week apart, though your plan depends on your condition. Improvement usually builds over weeks rather than right away. Your clinician should explain how progress will be measured.

### What is the difference between focused and radial shockwave?

Focused shockwave penetrates deeper at higher energy and targets a specific spot. Radial shockwave is more superficial and covers a wider area. The right choice depends on the tissue being treated and how deep it sits.

### Can I exercise after a shockwave session?

Recovery time is usually little to none, and many people return to normal activity. Ask your clinician for specific guidance on running, lifting or training in the days after. Exercise and rehab are also an important part of getting lasting results.

## Shockwave Therapy for Knees in Plain Terms

Shockwave therapy goes by a few names. You will see "ESWT," which stands for extracorporeal shockwave therapy, and you will see "acoustic wave therapy." They describe the same basic idea.

A handheld device delivers pulses of energy through the skin to a sore area. Nothing is cut, injected or implanted. You stay awake, and you walk out on your own.

### What ESWT Is

Per a [clinical trial consent document](https://cdn.clinicaltrials.gov/large-docs/80/NCT06686680/ICF_001.pdf), ESWT is a non-invasive, handheld treatment used to relieve pain and promote healing in bones, tendons, ligaments and muscles. Gel goes on the skin, and the device is held against it.

The word "extracorporeal" simply means the waves are generated outside the body. They travel in through the skin to the tissue underneath.

For knees, that tissue is usually one of these:

- The patellar tendon, just below the kneecap
- The quadriceps tendon, just above the kneecap
- Soft tissue around the joint line
- The joint area itself, when arthritis is the driver of pain

### Focused Vs. Radial Shockwave

Not every shockwave device works the same way. The [same trial document](https://cdn.clinicaltrials.gov/large-docs/80/NCT06686680/ICF_001.pdf) explains that a radial device produces a pressure wave that strikes the treated area. A focused device produces a sound wave that sends energy into the injured area.

In plain terms, radial waves tend to act on the surface and a broad area. Focused waves are aimed to concentrate energy at a chosen depth.

Which one matters for knees? Researchers are still sorting that out. A [network meta-analysis](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8869515/) of knee osteoarthritis trials notes that the difference in effect between radial and focused shockwave at different energy levels remains controversial.

That is useful to know. Be wary of any page that tells you one type clearly wins. The honest answer is that the evidence does not yet settle it.

## How Shockwave Therapy Is Thought to Work

Here is where honesty matters. Clinics often describe the mechanism with great confidence. Researchers are more careful.

A [review of ESWT in knee osteoarthritis](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7670564/) says shockwaves can be generated by electrohydraulic, electromagnetic or piezoelectric sources. It also says the exact mechanism by which shockwave therapy works remains unclear.

So what follows are the leading ideas, not proven facts.

### Pain Signaling, Blood Flow and Tissue Response

The commonly proposed explanations fall into three groups:

1. **Pain signaling.** The waves may change how nerves in the area send pain messages, which could lower sensitivity for a time.
2. **Blood flow.** The waves may encourage new small blood vessels to form, which could support circulation to tissue that heals slowly.
3. **Tissue response.** The mechanical stimulus may nudge irritated or stalled tissue back into a repair process.

Tendons are a good example of why this idea appeals. Many tendons have limited blood supply, and a stubborn tendon problem can linger for months. A treatment that stimulates the area is attractive in theory.

Theory is not the same as proof, though. Keep that in mind as we look at the evidence.

### What Science Still Does Not Know

Researchers are still working out several basic questions:

- Exactly which biological steps produce pain relief
- The best energy level for each knee condition
- How many pulses per session help most
- How many sessions are ideal
- Whether focused or radial delivery is better for a given problem

That last point is not a reason to dismiss shockwave therapy. It is a reason to treat it as one tool, to test how your knee responds, and to pair it with movement work.

## Which Knee Problems Shockwave Therapy Is Used For

Knee pain is not one problem. A sore tendon and an arthritic joint behave differently, and the evidence differs for each. Separating them is the most useful thing you can do before deciding.

### Tendon Pain (Patellar Tendinopathy)

Patellar tendinopathy, often called jumper's knee, causes pain at the front of the knee, just below the kneecap. It shows up in people who jump, sprint, squat or change direction a lot.

It can also affect weekend athletes and lifters who add volume too quickly. The tendon becomes irritated and then struggles to tolerate load.

Shockwave therapy is commonly offered for this problem. The research, however, is mixed. We cover it in the evidence section below.

If you are a runner and your pain sits lower, near the heel, our guide to [shockwave therapy for Achilles tendonitis](https://www.movemuscleandjoint.com/blog/shockwave-therapy-for-achilles-tendonitis) covers a related tendon problem.

### Joint Pain (Knee Osteoarthritis)

Knee osteoarthritis is wear and change in the joint. It tends to cause stiffness after rest, aching with activity and a sense that the knee no longer tolerates what it used to.

Here the research leans more positive, especially for mild to moderate cases. Studies have looked at pain and function after a short course of treatment.

Shockwave therapy does not rebuild a worn joint. What it may do is reduce pain enough that you can move and strengthen the muscles that support the knee.

### Where the Evidence Is Thin

Some situations have little support. A 2024 [randomized trial](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11254909/) states there is insufficient evidence for ESWT in grade IV knee osteoarthritis, the most advanced stage.

If your imaging shows severe, bone-on-bone change, shockwave therapy is unlikely to be the main answer. A conversation with your doctor about other options makes sense.

Post-surgical knees are another area where you should ask your surgeon first. Do not start any new treatment after a knee procedure without that clearance.

## Does Shockwave Therapy for Knees Work? What the Research Says

The fair answer is: sometimes, depending on the problem, and the research is not as tidy as sales pages suggest.

### Knee Osteoarthritis

The picture here is encouraging but limited. The [review of ESWT in knee osteoarthritis](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7670564/) still says evidence of definite effectiveness remains insufficient.

Small studies do report good tolerance. In a [pilot trial of mild knee osteoarthritis](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10659666/), all 18 patients completed 3 sessions with no complications. The trial was small, so treat it as a first signal, not proof.

A [randomized trial of 125 patients](https://www.mdpi.com/2227-9032/11/21/2830) found that moderate energy flux density, between 0.12 and 0.25 mJ/mm², seemed to work better. It found no difference between 2,000 and 4,000 pulses per session.

What that tells us is that dose matters, and that more pulses are not automatically better. A provider who adjusts settings to your response is doing what the research points toward.

### Patellar Tendinopathy

Here the data are less friendly. A [systematic review and meta-analysis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37662911/) found that in the short term, ESWT, with or without eccentric exercise, had a negligible effect on pain and function compared with placebo.

That does not mean no one benefits. It means the average result in the studies reviewed was small in the short term.

For jumper's knee, this is why the plan matters more than the device. Progressive loading of the tendon is the foundation. Shockwave therapy may be an add-on, not the core of care.

### Why Study Results Vary

It helps to know why trials disagree. Common reasons include:

- Small groups of participants
- Different devices and energy levels
- Different numbers of sessions and pulses
- Different ways of measuring pain and function
- Different stages of the same condition

When you read a claim like "high success rate" on a clinic page, ask what was measured, in whom, and for how long. Good providers can answer plainly.

### What the Research Does Not Say

No study we have cited shows that shockwave therapy cures knee arthritis or tendinopathy. None supports a promise of permanent relief. Some reviews suggest benefit can last for months, but protocols and results vary.

The honest framing is this: shockwave therapy may help some people reduce pain and move better, especially when paired with the right exercises. Lasting results come from changing how the knee is loaded and moved.

## What a Shockwave Session Feels Like

Knowing what to expect takes most of the worry out of a first treatment. Here is the usual flow.

### During Treatment

The provider applies gel to the skin over the sore area. Then they move the handheld device over or around the tender spots, delivering pulses as they go.

The [clinical trial document](https://cdn.clinicaltrials.gov/large-docs/80/NCT06686680/ICF_001.pdf) notes that patients may feel pressure, vibration or pain during treatment, and that the machine is loud.

Most people describe a tapping or pulsing feeling, and the sensation can be sharper over tender tissue. Tell your provider if it is too much. Settings can be adjusted.

### How Long It Takes

A clinic page from [Avenue Clinic](https://avenueclinic.co.uk/shockwave-therapy-for-knee-arthritis-can-it-help-you-move-better/) reports sessions of 20 to 30 minutes. At Move Muscle & Joint, shockwave therapy is one part of a full visit that may also include movement assessment, rehab guidance and hands-on care.

### Typical Courses of Treatment

Research protocols often use a short series of sessions spaced about a week apart. One narrative review describes 3 to 5 weekly sessions in many studies, though protocols vary.

Your own plan depends on your knee, your response and your goals. We do not promise a set number up front. We check how you move and feel at each visit and adjust.

### After the Session

You can usually walk out and go about your day. Your provider may suggest light activity, and may ask you to hold off on hard workouts for a short period.

Expect to hear specific guidance for your knee. It should include what to do for movement, not just what to avoid.

## Safety, Side Effects and Who Should Avoid It

Shockwave therapy is generally described as well tolerated, but it is not for everyone, and some soreness is common.

### Common Side Effects

[Avenue Clinic](https://avenueclinic.co.uk/shockwave-therapy-for-knee-arthritis-can-it-help-you-move-better/) reports soreness for 1 to 2 days, along with mild swelling or bruising. Other reviews describe mild, temporary redness or soreness.

These effects tend to be short-lived. For more detail, read our guide to [shockwave therapy side effects](https://www.movemuscleandjoint.com/blog/shockwave-therapy-side-effects).

### Who Should Be Cautious

The same clinic page lists reported cautions. Confirm these with your provider or doctor before treatment:

- Pregnancy
- A blood clotting disorder
- A metal implant near the treatment area

Tell your provider about any medications, past knee surgery, injections and other health conditions. A good provider will ask before treating.

### When to See a Doctor Promptly

Shockwave therapy is not the place to start if something more serious could be going on. See a doctor promptly if you have:

- A hot, red, swollen knee, especially with a fever
- Pain after a fall or injury with inability to bear weight
- Numbness, tingling or weakness in the leg
- A knee that locks, gives way or looks deformed
- Loss of bladder or bowel control
- Calf pain and swelling that came on suddenly

These signs call for medical evaluation first, not a new treatment.

## Is Shockwave Therapy Right for Your Knee?

Use this checklist as a starting point. It is not a diagnosis, and your provider should confirm.

### Signs It May Be Worth Considering

- Your pain has lasted weeks or months and is not settling with rest
- The pain is at a tendon, such as just below the kneecap
- You have mild to moderate knee arthritis and want a non-surgical option
- You are willing to pair treatment with exercise
- You do not have the cautions listed above

### Signs to Slow Down

- Your knee is hot, swollen or recently injured
- You have advanced, grade IV arthritis
- You are pregnant, have a clotting disorder or have an implant near the knee
- You are recovering from knee surgery and have not been cleared
- You want a single treatment to replace all movement work

### Questions to Ask Any Provider

Before any ESWT course, you can ask:

1. What do you think is causing my knee pain?
2. Why is shockwave therapy suited to this problem?
3. What else will be part of my plan?
4. How will we know whether it is working?
5. What happens if it is not?

A clear, honest answer to these is a good sign. Vague promises are not.

## Does Shockwave Therapy Help Achilles Tendonitis?

Short answer: it may help some people, mostly those with pain in the middle of the tendon, when it is added to an exercise program. The evidence is mixed, and exercise stays the foundation.

Shockwave therapy, also called ESWT (extracorporeal shockwave therapy), sends acoustic wave pulses through the skin into the sore tendon. It is an add-on, not a stand-alone fix.

I would treat it as a second-line option. That means you try a structured loading plan first. If progress stalls, shockwave can be a reasonable next step inside that plan.

Here is the honest summary of where things stand:

- Reviews from 2022 found useful benefit for midportion pain when shockwave was added to loading exercises.
- Newer 2026 analyses of sham-controlled trials are more cautious, and one stopped short of recommending it.
- Evidence for pain at the heel end of the tendon is weaker than for pain in the middle.
- No review shows that shockwave replaces exercise.

That is not a reason to dismiss it. It is a reason to set honest expectations and pair it with movement.

### Where Reviews Disagree

A [2022 systematic review of randomized trials](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9106789/) found that adding shockwave to a tendon loading program gave a clinically important improvement on the VISA-A score for midportion cases. It seemed ineffective for insertional cases.

A [2022 review in Cureus](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9382436/) reported consistent evidence from four randomized trials that ESWT is effective for midportion Achilles tendinopathy.

Newer work is less upbeat. A [January 2026 meta-analysis of eight trials](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12786123/) with 458 participants compared ESWT with sham treatment and described effectiveness as still debated.

A [2026 meta-analysis in JOSPT](https://www.jospt.org/doi/10.2519/jospt.2026.13985) pooled nine trials of shockwave versus sham. Its authors wrote that they cannot recommend shockwave for either insertional or midportion Achilles tendinopathy.

Why the gap? Reviews differ in which trials they include, how they compare against sham, and whether patients also did exercise. Sham treatments can also produce real improvement, which narrows the measured difference.

My takeaway: shockwave is not a proven stand-alone solution. It is a reasonable option for stubborn cases, and the decision should rest on your response, not on hype.

## Achilles Tendonitis Vs. Tendinopathy

You will hear both words. "Tendonitis" suggests inflammation. Many long-lasting Achilles problems are better described as tendinopathy, a broader term for a tendon that hurts and has lost some of its capacity to handle load.

The [Cureus review](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9382436/) explains that tendinopathy covers paratenonitis, which is inflammation of the tissue around the tendon, and tendinosis, which is degeneration without inflammation.

Why does the word matter? If the tendon needs to rebuild strength, anti-inflammatory approaches alone rarely solve the problem. Gradual loading usually does the heavy lifting.

### Midportion Achilles Pain

Midportion pain sits roughly two to six centimeters above the heel bone. The tendon may feel thick or tender when you pinch it. Mornings and the start of a run are often the worst.

### Insertional Achilles Pain

Insertional pain sits right where the tendon attaches to the heel bone. It often flares with hill running, deep ankle bends and tight shoes that press on the back of the heel.

The two types respond differently to treatment. Insertional cases often need changes to how you stretch and load the tendon, such as avoiding deep heel drops early on.

### How Common Is It in Runners?

A [study of sex differences in runners](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11641900/) reported lifetime Achilles tendinopathy rates of 31% in elite short-distance runners and 42% in elite middle- and long-distance runners before age 45.

If you run in Johnson County and your Achilles hurts, you are in common company. Common does not mean you should ignore it.

## What Shockwave Therapy Is and How It Works

Shockwave therapy uses acoustic wave pulses delivered through a handheld applicator. The pulses travel into the tendon and surrounding tissue. The clinical goal is to calm pain and support the tendon's response to loading.

The exact mechanism is still being studied. I would be wary of any page that explains it with total certainty.

### Radial Vs. Focused

You may see the terms radial and focused. Radial devices spread energy across a wider, shallower area. Focused devices concentrate energy at a set depth.

Some Achilles trials used radial shockwave. That matters when you read research, because results from one type do not automatically apply to the other. Ask any provider what type of device they use and why.

### During Treatment

A typical session is simple. Here is what to expect:

1. Your provider checks the tendon, your history and your training load.
2. Gel goes on the skin over the sore area.
3. The applicator delivers pulses over the tendon for several minutes.
4. You and your provider adjust intensity based on what you can tolerate.
5. You review a movement plan before you leave.

The sensation is often described as tapping or pressure that can feel sore over tender spots. An [NHS leaflet from Oxford](https://www.ouh.nhs.uk/media/on3fiyfn/86722shockwave.pdf) says patients may need up to three sessions of about 10 minutes each.

## Does It Work? Evidence by Tendon Location

Location matters more than most pages admit. The evidence is not the same for the middle of the tendon and the heel attachment.

### Midportion

For midportion pain, the [2022 randomized trial review](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9106789/) found a clinically important gain when shockwave was added to loading. The Cureus review also leaned positive.

The 2026 sham-controlled analyses push back. So the fair statement is this: shockwave may help some people with midportion pain, but the strength of the evidence is debated.

### Insertional

For insertional pain, evidence is thinner and less encouraging. The 2022 review described shockwave as seemingly ineffective here.

A [randomized trial of radial shockwave](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12816402/) in people with insertional pain looked at immediate pain changes with movement. It found radial shockwave no more effective than sham for that immediate outcome.

That does not mean nothing helps insertional pain. It means load management, footwear changes and progressive strengthening deserve most of your attention.

### Who May Respond Less

A 2024 retrospective study of 88 runners with Achilles or hamstring tendinopathy treated with ESWT, in the [sex differences paper](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11641900/), found that females taking oral contraceptives were less likely to reach a minimal clinically important difference.

This was a retrospective study, so it shows an association, not a cause. It does suggest that individual response varies, which is one reason a good plan should not hinge on one tool.

## Exercise First: Why Loading Is the Foundation

Tendons get stronger when they are loaded in a gradual, planned way. That is why exercise sits at the center of nearly every credible Achilles plan, and shockwave is positioned as a secondary option for stubborn cases.

One review describes shockwave as a secondary conservative treatment for refractory tendinopathies, meaning cases that have not settled with first-line care.

A good loading plan usually includes:

- Calf raises with the knee straight and bent, starting with a range you can control.
- Slow, heavy repetitions that build tolerance over weeks, not days.
- Single-leg work once two-leg work feels solid.
- Hopping and running drills later, when strength and pain allow.

The load, speed and range change by person. Insertional cases often start with a smaller range. A provider should adjust this based on your response, not a fixed recipe.

### How a Combined Plan Works

In a combined plan, shockwave is one piece. The rest looks like this:

3. **Movement retraining** for how you land, push off and spread effort across the leg and hip.
4. Hands-on care such as myofascial release therapy for tight calf and foot tissue when it helps you move better.

## How Many Sessions and How Long Recovery Takes

Most protocols use a short series. The NHS leaflet mentions up to three sessions. Clinic pages commonly describe about three treatments spaced a week apart, though your plan may differ.

Sessions are only part of the timeline. The tendon needs weeks of loading to adapt. Here is a realistic picture:

- **Early weeks:** pain may start to settle as you reduce aggravating load and start strengthening.
- **Four to six weeks:** one hospital page, [Mercy](https://mdmercy.com/mercy-services/treatments/shock-wave-therapy-for-achilles-tendonitis-and-plantar-faciitis), puts early healing in this window.
- **Several months:** the same page notes recovery can continue for months.

That timeline comes from a hospital service page, not from a trial, so treat it as a rough guide. Chronic cases can take longer.

### Signs You Are on Track

- Morning stiffness shortens over time.
- You can do more calf raises or heavier loads without a flare.
- Walking, stairs and short jogs feel more normal.
- Pain the next morning stays steady or settles instead of climbing.

If you see no change after a fair trial of a combined plan, say so. Your provider should adjust the plan, revisit the diagnosis or refer you on.

## Can Runners Keep Running During Treatment?

Often, yes, with limits. Many runners can keep moving if pain stays low and settles quickly. Complete rest tends to leave the tendon weaker, so the aim is to find a load you can tolerate.

This is general guidance, not a prescription. Your plan should match your history and your tendon.

### A Pain-Monitoring Approach

I would use a simple check. Rate your pain from 0 to 10 during and after activity, then check again the next morning.

- Mild discomfort that stays low and settles is usually acceptable.
- Pain that climbs during a run, or sharp pain that forces a change in stride, is a signal to stop.
- A worse morning after a run means you did too much. Cut back next time.
- Trend matters more than any single day.

Cut back volume or speed before cutting everything. Swapping some runs for cycling or pool work keeps fitness while the tendon adapts.

### Return-to-Run Progression

If you stopped running, return in stages instead of jumping to your old routine:

1. Walk comfortably and complete your strength work with good form.
2. Add short run and walk intervals on flat ground.
3. Increase running time before adding speed.
4. Add hills and speed work last, since they load the tendon most.

Move up a step only when the last one felt steady and the next morning was calm. Give each stage several sessions.

## Side Effects and Who Should Skip It

Shockwave is generally well tolerated, but it is not risk-free. The NHS leaflet lists these possible effects:

- Pain during treatment and for up to 24 hours afterward.
- Skin reddening.
- Swelling.
- Bruising.
- Calf aching and numbness.
- A very rare small risk of Achilles rupture.

For more detail, see our guide to shockwave therapy side effects.

A provider should screen you before treatment. Tell them about recent steroid injections, blood thinners, pregnancy, implants near the area or any history of a tendon tear. Some conditions mean treatment should wait or be skipped.

### When to See a Doctor Promptly

Get medical care quickly instead of waiting for a treatment plan if you notice:

- A sudden pop or snap in the back of the ankle, or sudden inability to push off.
- Marked swelling, a visible gap or severe bruising in the tendon.
- Fever, redness and warmth around the heel or calf.
- Calf pain with swelling in one leg, which can signal a clot.
- New numbness, weakness or loss of bladder control.

These can point to a rupture, infection, clot or another problem that needs a doctor.

## Frequently asked questions

### Does shockwave therapy cure Achilles tendonitis?

No treatment can promise a cure. Shockwave therapy may help some people with midportion pain when it is added to a loading program, but recent reviews disagree on how strong the effect is. Lasting results usually come from rebuilding tendon strength and managing training load.

### How many shockwave sessions do I need for my Achilles?

Many protocols use a short series, and an NHS leaflet mentions up to three sessions of about 10 minutes each. Your provider may adjust that based on your tendon and how you respond. Recovery usually takes weeks to months, because the tendon needs time to adapt to loading.

### Is shockwave therapy for the Achilles painful?

It can feel sore, especially over tender spots, and intensity can be adjusted. Pain during treatment and for up to 24 hours afterward is a listed side effect, along with redness, swelling, bruising and calf aching. Tell your provider if the sensation is more than you can tolerate.

### Can I keep running while I get shockwave therapy?

Often yes, in a reduced and monitored way. Keep pain low, check how the tendon feels the next morning, and cut back if it flares. Your provider can build a return-to-run plan that fits your tendon type and training history.
